# Troy School District — Structural Deficit Analysis

**Working analysis as of 2026-05-13. Sources: TSD ACFRs FY15-FY24 (Plante Moran), FY25 audit (Plante Moran), FY26 4-year projection (Feb 2026), Oct 2025 SpEd Update presentation, FY11-FY26 check register reconciliation (224K line items, $1.227B), Chalkbeat MI SpEd funding report (Oct 2025).**

---

## TL;DR

Troy School District faces a structural operating deficit projected at **$5-12M/year** through FY29. The board's superintendent has referenced **$12M in FY27 cuts** as the calibration to restore the 15% fund balance target.

The deficit is **not primarily a Covid hangover or one-time event**. It is the cumulative result of four structural drivers:

1. **Special Education cost growth outpacing reimbursement.** SpEd expenditures grew +33% in 4 years (FY21 → FY25, $25.6M → $34.0M) while reimbursement-source revenue grew slower. The General Fund now subsidizes SpEd by **~$9M/year**, up from ~$6M in FY21.

2. **Per-pupil operating cost grew 31% in 5 years** ($11,766 in FY19 → $15,383 in FY24), compounding ~5.5%/year — well above inflation and well above the foundation-allowance growth rate.

3. **ESSER cliff.** Federal revenue surged $7M → $21M FY19-FY22 (~$30M cumulative one-time), supporting expanded staffing and programs that became baseline. Federal revenue is back to $13M in FY24 and falling — but the staffing didn't snap back.

4. **Enrollment decline.** Headcount fell from peak 13,073 (FY20) to 12,447 (FY24), -626 students / -4.8%. District is now budgeting 200 FTE/year continuing declines through FY29 — each 100-FTE loss is ~$1M of foundation revenue.

These overlay a statewide structural problem: Michigan reimburses only **28.6%** of district SpEd costs (one of the lowest rates nationally), forcing all districts to subsidize SpEd from operating revenue intended for general instruction.

### The Levenson engagement — full report obtained and read

- **Vendor**: New Solutions K12 (Nathan Levenson, formerly District Management Council). Brookline, MA.
- **Report title**: *"Improving Outcomes and Equity for Students with Disabilities and Other Students who Struggle"* — Troy School District — **December 2023** (32 pages)
- **Engagement timeline**: July 2023 invoices through April 2025
- **Total fees**: $325,000 across 20 invoices
- **Booked to**: General Fund Function 221 (Improvement of Instruction), Budget Unit 101425221000000, Account 3190 (Other Purchased Services)
- **Phase shift**: Invoices labeled "CONSULTING" Jul 2023 - Nov 2024; shifted to "SP ED IMPLEMENTATION / SP ED INTEGRATION" from Dec 2024 onward — implementation phase confirmed
- **Methodology**: 130+ stakeholder interviews, classroom visits, benchmark analysis, staff time-study sharing

**What the Levenson report actually says (vs. public narrative):**

The report's 5 commendations and 5 opportunities are primarily about **instructional quality and outcomes for students with mild-moderate disabilities** — not about adding 1:1 health-care aides.

**Commendations:**
1. Teachers are hardworking and dedicated
2. District has community confidence
3. Deep understanding of reading best practices (elementary)
4. Elementary Multi-Tiered System of Support (MTSS) well defined
5. **"The district has the desire and ability to serve students with severe needs in-district with its broad range of supports and services... very few students are required to attend out of district programs."**

Commendation #5 is dispositive: as of December 2023, TSD was ALREADY serving severe-needs students in-district. The Levenson engagement did NOT trigger a wave of in-sourcing — it documented an in-sourcing status quo that already existed.

**Opportunities (Levenson's recommendations):**
1. Expand elementary literacy specialists' reach (away from 1:1, toward small groups of 5)
2. Build general ed teacher capacity for mild-moderate disabilities (away from "your students vs. my students")
   - 2A. Use the SAME curriculum and standards for SpEd as gen ed
   - 2B. Expand instructional coaching (literacy specialists currently spend only 12% of time coaching)
   - 2C. Move academics for ALL students under Teaching & Learning org; leave only severe-needs, related services, compliance, and paraprofessional management under SpEd
3. Comprehensive elementary schedules ("flood the grade" intervention blocks)
4. Best-practice secondary interventions (content-strong teachers, double-block, 15-student groups)
5. Tighten cross-school alignment

**The critical staffing quote (page 11):** *"The district currently spends over two million dollars ($2,000,000) on student support aides across the district, plus 100 contracted full-time paraprofessionals. Every special education resource room is currently staffed with at least one full-time student support aide and every categorical room has at least two full-time paraprofessionals."*

**Levenson's recommended direction on aides (page 11):** *"If the support model shifts from pulling students with disabilities into a resource room for reading support to keeping them in the general education classroom plus receiving support from teachers with content expertise (i.e., Literacy Specialists and highly skilled special education teachers), **fewer paraprofessionals or student support aides will be needed**. All shifts in staff should be thoughtfully managed through natural attrition and no one should lose their job as a result of these changes."*

**This is the inverse of what the financial data shows happened.** Levenson recommended attrition-based REDUCTION in paraprofessional usage. Edustaff HCA spending instead grew from $1.33M (FY23) → $2.66M (FY25), and the in-house TESPA bargaining unit headcount (107 FTE per FY26 budget) didn't decline. The Levenson recommendations on aide-attrition were not implemented at the staffing level, even as the consulting engagement continued into "implementation" mode through April 2025.

**Critical fact**: Health Care Aide spending **pre-dates Levenson** ($1.33M in FY23 vs. Levenson engagement starting Jul 2023). HCA costs were already accelerating when New Solutions K12 was retained. Combined with Levenson's recommendation to REDUCE aides, the conclusion is clear: Levenson was brought in to *contain* a SpEd cost spiral that had already begun, AND his containment advice on staffing was not followed.

### Did TSD in-source severe-needs students? (refined answer — partial yes, but the math doesn't work as cost savings)

The hypothesis: TSD previously sent cognitively-impaired and severe-needs students to out-of-district programs (Wing Lake at Bloomfield Hills SD, county center programs at Oakland Schools, Severin Intermediate, Huron Valley), then "brought them home" to save tuition, with HCAs covering the in-district staffing.

**The data shows a partial in-sourcing signal — but the magnitude doesn't support a cost-saving rationale.**

Out-of-district payment trajectory shows real movement around FY22-FY23:

| FY | Fund 101 Out-of-District SpEd Total |
|---|---:|
| FY18 | $787K |
| FY19 | $709K |
| FY20 | $465K (Covid) |
| FY21 | $851K |
| **FY22** | **$1,381K** ← peak |
| **FY23** | **$631K** ← -$750K vs FY22 |
| FY24 | $804K |
| FY25 | $753K |
| FY26 YTD | $532K |

The FY22→FY23 drop of $750K is real and driven by:
- **Severin Intermediate**: $295K (FY22) → $0 (FY23) — ended entirely
- **Oakland Schools ISD**: $704K (FY22) → $214K (FY23) — dropped $490K

So there IS an in-sourcing pattern. But the magnitudes don't work as a cost-saving story:

- **Cumulative HCA (Account 3116) spend FY23-FY25:** $6.5M
- **Cumulative out-of-district savings vs FY22 peak:** ~$2M
- **Net cost increase:** ~$4.5M

If the rationale was "in-source to save money," the math fails by a wide margin. The HCA addition costs ~3x what the out-of-district reduction saves.

**Three plausible alternative interpretations:**

1. **Demand-driven, framed as values-driven.** TSD experienced increased severe-needs enrollment (national post-Covid autism/behavioral trend) and didn't have capacity at county center programs (Wing Lake, etc.) to absorb all of it. Forced to expand in-house Level 4 programs (20 programs as of FY25, including 15 elementary). Now describes this as "we proudly serve severe-needs in-district," but the choice was constrained, not voluntary. Levenson's Commendation #5 (Dec 2023) ratifies this as a values-driven strength — which may serve as PR cover for what was actually capacity/compliance-driven expansion.

2. **Hidden county millage costs.** Out-of-district county center program tuition is largely funded through Oakland County's PA-18 SpEd millage, which doesn't flow through TSD's check register. TSD pays into the millage levy regardless of how many students it sends. So "saving out-of-district tuition" may have been illusory — TSD couldn't actually save county millage costs by bringing kids home, only the small direct-bill items we see ($750K).

3. **Compliance forcing in-house programming.** Post-Endrew F. (2017 US Supreme Court), IEP teams under more pressure to provide FAPE in the least restrictive environment (LRE) — which favors in-district placement over sending to county center programs. TSD may have been pushed by IEP teams and due-process settlements to bring students back. The Levenson report's emphasis on "general ed first" (Opportunity 2A: same standards/curriculum) reflects this LRE framework.

### Did TSD lay off SpEd staff and replace with contractors? (refined answer — partial yes, with major caveats)

**The "lay off and replace" pattern IS clearly visible — but for OPERATIONS, not Special Education.**

From ACFR FTE data:
- Support Services FTE collapsed FY17→FY19: 308 → 162 = **-146 FTE in 2 years**
- DM Burr Facilities (outsourced custodial): $3.2M (FY19) → $5.8M (FY25), +$2.7M
- First Group America (outsourced transportation): $2.9M → $4.0M, +$1.1M

This is a textbook lay-off-and-replace pattern: district eliminated ~146 custodial/operations FTE around FY18, replaced with managed-services contracts. Cost-neutral or slight savings (district avoided MPSERS liability on those positions); operationally more flexible.

**For Special Education specifically, the picture is more complex:**

Total contract staffing across all funds and vendors:

| FY17 | $6.0M | Professional Ed Services $5.4M |
| FY18 | $6.2M | Professional Ed Services $3.1M + Edustaff $2.2M |

| FY20 | $1.2M | Covid |
| FY21 | $2.0M | Edustaff recovering |

| FY25 | $5.6M | Edustaff |

**TSD has used $4-6M/yr in contract staff for a decade.** Current levels are AT historical peak, not above it. The FY19-FY21 trough was the anomaly (vendor consolidation + Covid). The "post-Levenson contract staffing explosion" narrative obscures this longstanding pattern.

**What IS new:** Account 3116 (Student Support Aide — Health Care Aide) went $0 (FY22) → $2.66M (FY25). This is a structurally new staffing role — 1:1 medical/behavioral aides — added on top of pre-existing contract-staffing use. Not a replacement, an addition.

**The TESPA bargaining unit (107 FTE in FY26 budget, district-employed paraprofessionals/support):** We don't have a year-over-year FTE series for TESPA specifically. If pre-Covid TESPA was significantly larger and is now smaller while Edustaff grew, that would support "lay off and replace" for SpEd specifically. But based on Levenson's Dec 2023 observation ("$2M+ on student support aides plus 100 contracted full-time paraprofessionals"), it appears both district-paid AND contracted aides currently exist in significant numbers. This suggests an ADDITIVE model, not a replacement model, for SpEd staffing.

**Bottom-line on lay-off-and-replace:**
- For **operations/facilities**: Yes, clearly happened FY17-FY19. ~146 district FTE replaced with DM Burr + First Group + others. Predates Levenson by 5 years.
- For **SpEd paraprofessionals**: Likely a mixed model. Some district-paid (TESPA 107 FTE) + some contracted (~100 Edustaff per Levenson). The HCA addition is NEW spending, not replacement.

**Annual out-of-district SpEd-related spend: ~$640K/yr average, mostly stable**. Even if TSD had fully eliminated out-of-district payments (impossible), that's $640K/yr — vs. $2.66M of new HCA cost. The arithmetic doesn't work.

**Levenson's Commendation #5 (Dec 2023) confirms there was no in-sourcing wave**: "very few students are required to attend out of district programs" — that was the EXISTING state, not a recent change.

**What more likely drove the HCA spike (in order of weight):**

1. **Rising intensity of student needs** — the Oct 2025 SpEd Update describes "substantial increase in those support needs over the last few years," and the disability-eligibility breakdown (Autism 19%, Specific Learning Disability 19%, Other Health Impairment 16%, Cognitive Impairment 5%) shows a mix consistent with rising behavioral and medical complexity. This aligns with:
   - National post-Covid mental health surge among children
   - Rising autism diagnoses (Michigan Autism State Plan documents continued growth)
   - Michigan behavioral therapy waitlists: 73% of MI families wait 1+ months for autism services, often >1 year (per peer-reviewed needs assessment, PMC 2024) — meaning children needing private behavioral support sit in schools needing school-provided 1:1 aides

2. **Labor model shift, not service-volume shift** — Trudel/Levenson observed ~100 contracted Edustaff paraprofessionals were ALREADY in place by Dec 2023. The growth from $0 to $2.66M in HCA spend partly reflects a shift from district-employed aides (in the TESPA bargaining unit) to contracted Edustaff aides — same role, different payer mechanism. Contracted aides are faster to deploy (no hiring cycle), have no MPSERS pension cost, and provide more operational flexibility, but cost more per hour. Per Levenson: *"every special education resource room is currently staffed with at least one full-time student support aide and every categorical room has at least two full-time paraprofessionals."*

3. **IEP compliance pressure post-Endrew F.** — *Endrew F. v. Douglas County School District* (US Supreme Court, 2017) raised the FAPE (Free Appropriate Public Education) standard. The case held that IEPs must be "reasonably calculated to enable a child to make progress appropriate in light of the child's circumstances" — meaningfully ambitious, not merely de minimis. Cumulative effect over 6+ years: more due-process challenges, more 1:1 aide orders from IEP teams or mediation. This is a federal legal trend, not a Michigan-specific 2022-2024 change.

4. **No specific Michigan legislative mandate** — there is no Michigan statute or MARSE rule change in 2022-2024 that mandated 1:1 aides. MARSE Rule 340.1832 (Severe Multiple Impairment programs) has long required 1 teacher + 2 instructional aides for max 9 SMI students — unchanged. 1:1 aide assignments come from IEP team decisions for individual students, not state mandate.

5. **Michigan's $246M state SpEd boost (FY23) does NOT relieve districts** — per Citizens Research Council of Michigan (July 2022): the additional state SpEd appropriation "offsets what districts already spend from their general funds to cover these services" rather than expanding services. The state increase is consumed by base cost growth, not a tailwind for districts.

**Bottom line for the in-sourcing question:** TSD did not bring students back from out-of-district. The HCA growth represents (a) increased per-student support needs in the existing in-district population, (b) a labor model shift toward Edustaff contracted aides, and (c) cumulative IEP compliance pressure post-Endrew F. There is no Michigan-specific 2022-2024 legislation that mandated this growth.

## Part 6: MPSERS pension pass-through

The Michigan Public School Employees Retirement System is the largest single non-payroll line in the operating budget at **$29.3M/year** (FY26 projection).

### Structural shift in FY22

- **Pre-FY22**: District paid MPSERS contributions directly from district funds. Check register shows MPSERS payments of $15-35M/year through FY21.
- **FY22 onwards**: Michigan shifted bulk pension funding (especially Section 147/147a state contributions for UAAL) to direct state payment. Now appears in the district books as offsetting state revenue and pension expense — a wash.

Per the FY24 ACFR Management's Discussion and Analysis (p. 9): the 2024 State School Aid Act provides "an offset to the MPSERS UAAL expenditures that each district incurs" rather than a traditional foundation allowance increase, "resulting in a $498 per pupil equivalent for the School District in 2024-2025." For TSD that's roughly $6M of state revenue routed specifically to absorb pension UAAL costs — visible on both the revenue and expense sides of the operating statement.

The state pension subsidy is therefore **revenue-neutral but expenditure-inflating**. When we say revenue grew $34M from FY19 to FY24, a substantial portion of that growth is MPSERS pass-through, matched dollar-for-dollar on the expense side. Real "free" operating revenue growth was much smaller.

## Part 9: Statewide context (Chalkbeat, Oct 2025)

A state-commissioned report (mandated by Michigan Legislature in 2024) found:

- **Michigan reimburses only 28.6% of special education costs** — among the lowest rates nationally
- Districts must use general fund dollars when SpEd costs exceed the $10,421 per-pupil grant
- Disparities: "a child's access to appropriate supports depends far too much on where they live"
- **Proposed fix**: Tiered funding ($11K low-needs → $39K intensive-needs) + 80% reimbursement above $57,615/student threshold; ~$4.55B implementation over 6 years
- 1,000+ stakeholders consulted

**Troy is not an outlier.** The $9M/year general-fund SpEd subsidy is the local manifestation of a statewide structural problem. Solving it requires state-level reform; districts can only manage at the margin.

## Part 10: Synthesis — putting it together

### Why does TSD have a structural deficit?

In rough order of magnitude (FY19 → FY25 incremental annual cost):

| Driver | Approx FY19 → FY25 incremental annual cost | Comment |
|---|---:|---|
| Compensation (salary + benefits) growth | ~$25M | $145.5M FY26 base, growing ~$7M/yr unaided |
| Within comp: MPSERS expense (mostly state pass-through) | ~$15-20M | Revenue-neutral but visible on expense side |
| Within comp: Healthcare (MESSA + BCBS) | ~$5M | From $11M to $15.7M just in MESSA |
| Within comp: Salary inflation (CBA + step) | ~$5M | ~1%/yr base average, plus step movements |
| Special education non-payroll (HCAs, etc.) | ~$4M | $0 → $5M Edustaff, partly offset by reduced subs |
| Outsourced services (custodial, transport, security) | ~$5M | DM Burr + First Group + Sec Shield + MASB-SEG |
| Other operating | ~$4M | Supplies, utilities, materials inflation |
| **Total expense growth** | **~$38M** | |

vs. revenue growth FY19 → FY25 of ~$32M, of which:
- ~$15-20M is MPSERS state pass-through (offsetting expense)
- ~$10M is foundation allowance increases (state per-pupil aid up ~$1,000)
- ~$5M ESSER pulled forward (now cliff-falling)
- ~$5M property tax / state categorical / other

**Net "free" operating revenue growth FY19→FY25 was much less than $32M — perhaps $12-15M after netting MPSERS pass-through and ESSER cliff.** Expenses grew ~$20M more than free revenue. That's the structural deficit.

### Why now?

- The ESSER cliff (federal $21M peak → $13M FY24 → ~$6M FY25+) crystallized at the same time MPSERS rate-increases compressed (state is now proposing reductions in MPSERS aid, which would be revenue-neutral on expense but visible).
- Enrollment decline (-626 from FY20 peak) compounded.
- Pre-existing SpEd cost trend (already growing in FY22) accelerated post-Covid as IEP complexity rose.
- Fund balance was drawn down from 17.4% (FY25) to 13.6% (FY26) — the cushion is now gone.

### What changed structurally — and what didn't

**Changed:**
- Support Services FTE collapsed (276 → 175 over 9 years) as district outsourced facilities, transportation
- SpEd staffing model expanded — 152 in-house aides + ~80 contracted HCAs (estimated from $2.66M / ~$30K loaded cost)
- ESSER funded 30-50 incremental positions (visible in teaching FTE 837 → 887)
- Teacher salaries grew slowly (~0.8%/yr average) — TSD has been compensating via headcount, not per-person pay

**Did not change:**
- Total FTE roughly flat (1,072 FY15 → 1,078 FY24)
- Average teacher salary growth at half of inflation
- Operating millage at full 18 mills
- Foundation allowance growth largely matched statewide inflation

The deficit is therefore *not* an "everybody got rich" story. It's a *composition* story: more aides, more SpEd specialists, more contracted services, more benefit costs — financed in part by one-time federal money that's now gone.

### Key takeaways

1. **The deficit is a benefits-load story, not a salary story.** Salaries grew at general-inflation pace (3.2% CAGR). Total comp grew much faster due to benefits.

2. **MPSERS is the heaviest weight.** Pension contributions went from 30% to 41% of payroll (peaked 49% in FY23). This reflects decades of MPSERS underfunding being amortized through current-employer contributions. Most is state pass-through now, but inflates the expense base.

3. **OPEB is the bright spot.** Retiree healthcare contributions only grew 4.7%/yr because the plan reached full funding (105%) by FY23. Future OPEB contributions should remain modest.

4. **MESSA active health: structural 7-9%/yr inflation** with no mitigating offset.

5. **Outsourced services growth is exaggerated by baseline choice.** FY19 was an anomalous trough; using FY18 baseline, outsourced grew only +4.5% CAGR — roughly in line with overall operating expenditures. The *composition* changed (new HCA layer, new security/insurance lines), but total contract-staffing levels are at historical peak, not above it.

6. **The $16M/year retiree-benefits cost growth is structural and mostly outside the district's control.** The state-pass-through framework (Section 147c) is mitigating but not eliminating it.
